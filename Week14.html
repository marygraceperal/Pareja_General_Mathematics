SESSION 1 — Practical Problems with Right and Oblique Triangles

Daily objectives: Represent a practical situation with a triangle, identify the information given, select an appropriate relationship, and solve a contextual problem.

ACTIVITY

Represent

Present a triangular support with two known sides and an included angle. Learners sketch the situation and mark the known quantities before choosing a formula.

ANALYSIS

Formative check

Pairs compare diagrams. Discuss why a correct diagram can prevent choosing an inappropriate formula.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model a decision routine: sketch → identify known/unknown → classify right/oblique → choose relationship → substitute → calculate → interpret. Review A = ½ab sin C and Heron’s Formula where appropriate.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve practical triangle problems involving ladders, supports, triangular land, and distances. Require a labeled representation and final contextual statement.

Evidence-to-action: If learners select formulas before interpreting the diagram, require a “What is known? What is unknown?” table before the next problem.

SESSION 2 — Angles of Elevation and Depression

Daily objectives: Distinguish angles of elevation and depression, construct a right-triangle model, and solve a height or distance problem using trigonometric ratios.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Use a school-building or tree scenario. Learners identify the horizontal line, line of sight, observer, object, and angle shown in the situation.

ANALYSIS

Concept check

Compare an observer looking upward with one looking downward. Learners explain the difference between elevation and depression and locate the corresponding angle.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model a labeled right triangle. Select sine, cosine, or tangent from the known/unknown sides, solve, round appropriately, and state the contextual answer.

tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve a tower/tree height problem and a line-of-sight distance problem. Learners must draw the horizontal reference line.

Evidence-to-action: If learners place the angle incorrectly, return to the horizontal reference line and have them redraw before calculating.

SESSION 3 — Bearings in Navigation

Daily objectives: Interpret three-figure bearings, represent a navigation situation diagrammatically, and use trigonometric relationships to determine a missing distance or direction.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Give routes such as 060°, 135°, 225°, and 315°. Learners sketch each direction from a north reference and describe the general direction.

ANALYSIS

Error analysis

Compare a correct bearing diagram with one measured from the wrong reference direction. Discuss why bearings are measured clockwise from north.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model three-figure bearing notation, north reference, clockwise direction, conversion to a useful acute angle when appropriate, and construction of a triangle for calculation.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve navigation problems involving two points, a route bearing, and a known distance. Require a compass-style sketch before computation.

Evidence-to-action: If learners reverse a bearing, require them to draw N–E–S–W first and trace the clockwise turn from north.

SESSION 4 — Integrated Triangle, Elevation/Depression and Bearing Problems

Daily objectives: Select an appropriate mathematical model for a mixed practical situation, solve it accurately, justify the method, and evaluate the reasonableness of the result.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Sort mixed problem cards into: right/oblique triangle, elevation/depression, or bearing. Learners explain the clue that led to each classification.

ANALYSIS

Strategy comparison

Examine two solution paths for one problem. Identify which diagram, formula, and unit statement are supported by the given information.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis

Build a universal problem-solving routine: read → sketch → label → select relationship → calculate → check → interpret. Emphasize that the diagram is part of the mathematical evidence.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Complete an integrated navigation/line-of-sight problem independently. Use the result to make a practical statement about distance, height, or direction.

Evidence-to-action: Use the assessment evidence to classify learners for targeted reteaching, guided practice, or enrichment.

LEARNER GUIDE

Practical Applications of Triangles

Use diagrams and relationships to turn real situations into solvable mathematical models.

What You Will Learn

  • Solve practical right- and oblique-triangle problems.
  • Model angles of elevation and depression.
  • Interpret three-figure bearings.
  • Use diagrams to select appropriate trigonometric relationships.
  • Check whether answers make sense in context.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
Angle of elevationThe angle formed when the line of sight is above the horizontal line from the observer.
Angle of depressionThe angle formed when the line of sight is below the horizontal line from the observer.
BearingA direction measured clockwise from north, commonly written as a three-figure angle.
Line of sightThe straight path from an observer to the object being observed.
Right triangleA triangle containing a 90° angle.
Oblique triangleA triangle that is not a right triangle.

Key Ideas & Formulas

sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent
A = ½ab sin C
Heron: A = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)], where s = (a+b+c) ÷ 2

Bearing convention: begin at North and measure clockwise. Write the bearing with three digits, such as 060°, 135°, or 315°.

Problem-solving routine: Read → Sketch → Label → Select relationship → Substitute → Calculate → Check → Interpret.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Right triangle

A 10 m ladder makes a 30° angle with the ground. The vertical height h is opposite the angle.

sin 30° = h/10
h = 10(0.5) = 5 m

Example 2 — Angle of elevation

An observer is 20 m from the base of a vertical pole. The angle of elevation to the top is 35°. Let h be the height.

tan 35° = h/20
h = 20 tan 35° ≈ 20(0.7002) = 14.0 m

Example 3 — Bearing

A boat travels on a bearing of 060°. From north, it turns 60° clockwise toward east. The bearing is therefore represented as 060°. A bearing must be read from the north reference, not from the east-west axis.

Example 4 — Oblique triangle

A triangular panel has sides 8 m and 11 m with included angle 30°.

A = ½(8)(11)sin 30° = 44(0.5) = 22 m²

Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Drawing a triangle without labeling the known information.
  • Choosing a trigonometric ratio before identifying the side relationships.
  • Placing an elevation/depression angle away from the correct horizontal reference.
  • Reading a bearing from east instead of clockwise from north.
  • Writing 60° instead of the required three-figure bearing 060°.
  • Using an angle that is not included in A = ½ab sin C.
  • Forgetting units or reporting an unreasonable result without checking.

Real-World Connections

These ideas support surveying, construction, map reading, navigation, engineering, aviation, outdoor route planning, and estimating heights or distances that cannot be measured directly.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Choosing a formula before drawing the situation.
  • Confusing elevation and depression.
  • Measuring a bearing from east or another direction instead of north.
  • Reading bearings counterclockwise.
  • Failing to write three-figure bearings.
  • Using sine/cosine/tangent without identifying side relationships.
  • Accepting a numerical answer without checking its contextual meaning.

Remediation

  • Use a fixed “sketch → label → identify O/A/H → choose ratio” routine.
  • Provide partially labeled elevation/depression diagrams.
  • Practice bearings with a compass cross before introducing full navigation problems.
  • Use short error-analysis items to diagnose representation errors separately from calculation errors.
  • Require every learner to write a final answer with number, unit, and context.

Enrichment

  • Design a simple school-site surveying task involving a building height.
  • Create a navigation route using two or more bearings.
  • Compare two solution methods and justify the more efficient one.
  • Use a spreadsheet or geometry tool to verify selected trigonometric calculations.
