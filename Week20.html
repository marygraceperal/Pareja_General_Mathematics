SESSION 1 — Illustrating Random Sampling

Daily objectives: Explain why samples are used and illustrate how a random sample can be selected from a population.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Present a school investigation: “What proportion of Grade 11 learners prefer printed versus digital mathematics resources?” Learners identify the population and propose ways to select participants.

ANALYSIS

Formative check

Compare a simple random selection with selecting only nearby friends. Discuss how selection procedures can introduce bias.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Define population, sample, and random sampling. Illustrate simple random, systematic, stratified, and cluster sampling at an introductory level.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Learners identify the most appropriate sampling technique for several school and community investigations and justify each choice.

Evidence-to-action: If learners confuse “random” with “any convenient group,” return to the idea that selection should provide a defensible chance of inclusion according to the stated sampling design.

SESSION 2 — Appropriate Sample Size

Daily objectives: Determine an appropriate sample size for a stated investigation using a provided sample-size rule or formula and explain the role of population size and precision.

ACTIVITY

Estimate

Compare investigations involving a small school population and a much larger population. Learners predict whether the same sample size is automatically appropriate.

ANALYSIS

Reasoning

Discuss why sample size depends on the purpose, population, desired precision, confidence, variability, and available resources.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model a stated sample-size rule. When the investigation uses Slovin’s formula, n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²), identify N as population size and e as the allowable margin of error.

Slovin: n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²)

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Learners determine sample sizes from stated N and e values, round appropriately to a whole participant, and explain the decision.

Evidence-to-action: If learners substitute e instead of e², pause and require them to label every quantity before calculation and check the final sample size against the population.

SESSION 3 — Parameter, Statistic, Probability and Nonprobability Sampling

Daily objectives: Distinguish parameters from statistics and probability from nonprobability sampling techniques using contextual examples.

ACTIVITY

Classify

Sort statements such as “the average of all 1,200 learners” and “the average of 100 selected learners” into population measure or sample measure.

ANALYSIS

Error analysis

Examine common sampling choices such as convenience, purposive, quota, simple random, systematic, stratified, and cluster sampling. Identify which are probability techniques.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

A parameter describes a population; a statistic describes a sample. Probability sampling uses a known/randomized selection mechanism; nonprobability sampling does not give every population member a known probability of selection.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Learners classify scenarios and explain whether the reported number is a parameter or statistic and whether the sampling technique is probability or nonprobability.

Evidence-to-action: If learners classify based on the size of a number rather than whether it describes the population or sample, require them to underline the words “all” or “selected.”

SESSION 4 — Integrated Sampling Investigation

Daily objectives: Design a defensible sampling plan, determine an appropriate sample size under stated assumptions, and distinguish the resulting statistic from the population parameter.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Quick classification: population/sample, parameter/statistic, probability/nonprobability, then select a sampling design for a proposed investigation.

ANALYSIS

Strategy comparison

Compare two proposed sampling plans. Identify possible bias, feasibility concerns, and whether the sample size rule was applied correctly.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis

Use the routine: define population → define target variable → choose sampling technique → determine sample size → select sample → distinguish statistic from parameter → explain limitations.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Learners complete an integrated school-based investigation plan and defend the sampling method and sample size.

Evidence-to-action: Use the mastery evidence to identify whether the learner's difficulty is conceptual (sampling terminology), procedural (sample-size calculation), or evaluative (justifying a sampling design).
Evidence-to-action: Use mastery evidence to distinguish conceptual, procedural, and interpretation errors.

LEARNER GUIDE

Random Sampling and Sample Size

Learn how researchers select samples and make careful distinctions between population and sample information.

What You Will Learn

  • Explain population and sample.
  • Illustrate probability and nonprobability sampling.
  • Determine an appropriate sample size under stated assumptions.
  • Distinguish parameters from statistics.
  • Justify a sampling technique for an investigation.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
PopulationThe entire group about which an investigation seeks information.
SampleA subset of the population selected for study.
Random samplingA sampling process that uses a random mechanism to select members according to a defined design.
Probability samplingA sampling approach in which population members have a known or definable probability of selection under the design.
Nonprobability samplingA sampling approach in which selection is not based on a known probability mechanism.
ParameterA numerical characteristic describing a population.
StatisticA numerical characteristic calculated from a sample.
Sample sizeThe number of observations or participants included in a sample.

Common Sampling Techniques

TechniqueBasic ideaExample
Simple randomSelect members randomly from the entire population.Randomly select 50 learner IDs from a complete list.
SystematicSelect every k-th member after a random start.Select every 10th name after choosing a starting point.
StratifiedDivide the population into relevant strata, then sample within each.Sample learners from each grade level.
ClusterSelect whole groups or clusters, then study members within selected clusters.Randomly select several sections and survey their learners.
ConvenienceSelect those who are easiest to reach.Survey students who happen to be in the room.
PurposiveSelect participants because they meet a chosen purpose or criterion.Select mathematics coordinators for an expert interview.
QuotaFill specified subgroup quotas without requiring random selection within groups.Recruit a target number from several categories using available participants.

Key Ideas and Formula

First identify the population and the purpose of the investigation. Then choose a sampling design that is appropriate, feasible, and as defensible as possible.

When Slovin’s formula is specified: n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²)

N = population size; e = allowable margin of error expressed as a decimal; n = calculated sample size. Round the final participant count appropriately and never select more participants than the population contains.

Parameter → population     Statistic → sample

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Parameter or statistic?

The average score of all 500 Grade 11 learners is 78. Because the value describes the whole population, 78 is a parameter.

Example 2 — Sample size

A school has N = 500 learners and the stated allowable margin of error is e = 0.05. Using Slovin’s formula:

n = 500 ÷ [1 + 500(0.05)²] = 500 ÷ 2.25 ≈ 222.22

A practical whole-number sample would be 223 if rounding up to ensure the stated minimum.

Example 3 — Probability versus nonprobability

Randomly selecting 100 learner IDs from a complete list is a probability-sampling approach. Asking the first 100 available learners to respond is a nonprobability convenience approach.

Example 4 — Choosing a technique

If a school wants representation from each grade level, stratified sampling may be appropriate because grade level forms meaningful strata.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing population with sample.
  • Calling a statistic a parameter because its value is large or small.
  • Calling convenience sampling random simply because many people are involved.
  • Forgetting to square e when using Slovin’s formula.
  • Using a sample size larger than the population.
  • Choosing a technique without considering the investigation's purpose or population structure.

Real-World Connections

Sampling is used in school surveys, public opinion studies, market research, quality control, health investigations, and community research when studying every member of a population is impractical.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Confusing population and sample.
  • Calling any large group a random sample.
  • Confusing a parameter with a statistic.
  • Thinking convenience sampling is random because participants are selected quickly.
  • Forgetting e² in Slovin’s formula when that formula is specified.
  • Choosing a sampling technique without connecting it to the population structure.

Remediation

  • Use “ALL vs SELECTED” labels to distinguish population and sample.
  • Use “POPULATION → parameter” and “SAMPLE → statistic” as a retrieval cue.
  • Give sorting cards for probability versus nonprobability techniques.
  • Require learners to label N and e before substituting into a stated formula.
  • Use scenario comparison before assigning more numerical problems.

Enrichment

  • Design a school survey and defend its sampling plan.
  • Compare two sample-size assumptions and discuss the effect of changing allowable error.
  • Use a spreadsheet to calculate sample sizes and compare rounding decisions.
  • Investigate how sampling bias can affect conclusions even when a sample is large.
