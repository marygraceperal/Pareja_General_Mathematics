Weekly Learning Intentions

  • Identify the appropriate volume formula for common three-dimensional objects.
  • Calculate volume using consistent units.
  • Relate cubic units to capacity, including litres and millilitres.
  • Apply volume and capacity to practical quantity and cost-estimation problems.
  • Check whether a calculated quantity and cost are reasonable.
Evidence-informed cycle: retrieve → estimate → represent → explicitly model → guided practice → error analysis → independent application → mastery check → targeted response.

SESSION 1 — Volume of Prisms and Containers

Daily objectives: Identify dimensions of a prism, select the correct volume formula, and calculate volume with appropriate cubic units.

ACTIVITY

Estimate

Show a box or rectangular container. Learners estimate how much space it can hold, then identify length, width, and height.

ANALYSIS

Formative check

Compare two diagrams and discuss why all three dimensions are needed for a rectangular prism.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model V = lwh. Emphasize consistent units, substitution, cubic units, and a reasonableness check.

Rectangular prism: V = lwh

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve problems involving boxes, containers, and storage spaces. Learners state what the volume means in context.

Evidence-to-action: If learners omit one dimension, use a labeled 3D sketch and a “three dimensions check” before the next problem.

SESSION 2 — Capacity and Unit Relationships

Daily objectives: Convert between common volume and capacity units and apply the conversion appropriately to practical containers.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Compare a 1 L bottle with a 1 dm³ cube. Predict the relationship and discuss familiar litre and millilitre measurements.

ANALYSIS

Error analysis

Examine incorrect conversions and identify whether the error came from the unit relationship or from arithmetic.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Establish useful relationships: 1 L = 1,000 mL and 1 L = 1,000 cm³. Model converting only after identifying the required unit.

1 L = 1,000 mL = 1,000 cm³

APPLICATION

Practical task

Convert container capacities and compare them. Learners explain which container holds more and by how much.

Evidence-to-action: If learners confuse cm³ and mL, return to the equivalence 1 cm³ = 1 mL for water-like capacity contexts.

SESSION 3 — Volume and Cost Estimation

Daily objectives: Use volume or capacity to estimate quantities needed for a practical task and calculate an associated cost.

ACTIVITY

Contextualize

Present a flour-production or storage situation. Learners identify volume/capacity, amount required per batch, number of batches, and unit price.

ANALYSIS

Reasoning

Compare estimates with exact calculations. Discuss why an estimate should be made before committing to a quantity or cost.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model the chain: volume/capacity → quantity required → number of containers/batches → unit price → total cost.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve practical cost-estimation problems and justify whether the final estimate is reasonable.

Evidence-to-action: If learners jump directly to cost, require a quantity table before multiplication by price.

SESSION 4 — Integrated Volume, Capacity and Cost Problem

Daily objectives: Select an appropriate volume/capacity model, solve a multi-step practical problem, and communicate a justified cost estimate.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Sort situations into volume, capacity, or cost estimation: water tank, rice box, flour production, and container purchase.

ANALYSIS

Strategy comparison

Analyze a worked solution containing a unit error and identify where the reasoning went wrong.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis

Use the routine: understand → sketch → identify dimensions → choose formula → calculate volume → convert capacity if needed → estimate quantity/cost → check.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Complete an integrated container/cost problem independently and defend the reasonableness of the result.

Evidence-to-action: Use the mastery evidence to distinguish formula, unit-conversion, computation, and interpretation needs.

LEARNER GUIDE

Volume, Capacity and Cost Estimation

Use three-dimensional measurements to determine how much a container can hold and what practical quantities may cost.

What You Will Learn

  • Calculate volume of common three-dimensional objects.
  • Connect volume and capacity.
  • Convert between useful units.
  • Use volume/capacity in practical quantity and cost estimates.
  • Check answers for units and reasonableness.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
VolumeThe amount of three-dimensional space occupied by an object or enclosed by a container.
CapacityThe amount a container can hold, commonly expressed in litres or millilitres.
PrismA three-dimensional solid with congruent parallel bases.
Cubic unitA unit used to measure volume, such as cm³ or m³.
Cost estimateAn approximate or calculated amount needed to complete a practical task.

Key Formulas and Relationships

Rectangular prism: V = lwh
Cube: V = s³
Cylinder: V = πr²h
1 L = 1,000 mL = 1,000 cm³
Cost = quantity × price per unit

Always use consistent units before calculating volume or comparing quantities.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Rectangular container

A balik-bayan box measures 60 cm × 40 cm × 50 cm.

V = 60(40)(50) = 120,000 cm³ = 120 L

Example 2 — Cylinder

A cylindrical tank has radius 0.5 m and height 2 m.

V = π(0.5)²(2) = 0.5π ≈ 1.57 m³

Example 3 — Cost estimation

If a production process needs 6 kg of flour per batch and flour costs ₱48 per kg, then:

Cost = 6 × ₱48 = ₱288 per batch.

Example 4 — Capacity conversion

A container has a volume of 3,500 cm³.

3,500 cm³ ÷ 1,000 = 3.5 L

Common Mistakes

  • Using only two dimensions for a rectangular prism.
  • Writing cm instead of cm³ for volume.
  • Mixing metres and centimetres in one volume calculation.
  • Confusing 1,000 mL with 1,000 m².
  • Multiplying a price before determining the required quantity.
  • Ignoring whether a result is realistic for the container or task.

Real-World Connections

Volume and capacity are used in water storage, food production, packaging, shipping, household containers, construction, agriculture, and business cost planning.

  2. Capacity is the amount a container can hold, commonly expressed in litres or millilitres.

  4. V = s³.
  5. 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 mL.
  6. 4,000 ÷ 1,000 = 4 L.
  7. V = 50(30)(20) = 30,000 cm³ = 30 L.
  8. V = 12³ = 1,728 cm³.

  14. 1 L = 1,000 cm³, not 100 cm³.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Confusing volume, area, and capacity.
  • Using cm or cm² instead of cm³ for volume.
  • Omitting height in rectangular-prism volume.
  • Mixing units before calculation.
  • Confusing litres with square or cubic units.
  • Calculating cost before determining quantity.

Remediation

  • Use physical boxes and label length, width, and height.
  • Build volume with unit cubes to connect dimensions to cubic units.
  • Use a conversion ladder for L↔mL and cm³↔L.
  • Require a quantity table before every cost calculation.
  • Use short error-analysis tasks to isolate unit versus formula errors.

Enrichment

  • Design a household water-storage plan.
  • Compare container sizes and costs per litre.
  • Create a small production cost estimate involving ingredients or packaging.
  • Use a spreadsheet to compare container volume, capacity, quantity, and total cost.
