SESSION 1 — Types of Data

Daily objectives: Identify and classify data as qualitative or quantitative, and distinguish discrete from continuous quantitative data.

ACTIVITY

Elicit

Present classroom examples: favorite subject, number of siblings, height, section, and daily study time. Learners classify each before formal definitions.

ANALYSIS

Formative Check

Compare two classifications. Ask: Is the variable a category or a numerical measurement? If numerical, is it countable or measurable?

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling

Model the classification tree: data → qualitative/quantitative → for quantitative, discrete/continuous.

Discrete = counted values.
Continuous = measured values that can take values within a range.

APPLICATION

Guided → Independent

Learners classify 10 school and community variables and justify two classifications in one sentence each.

Evidence-to-action: If learners classify all numerical data as discrete, contrast “number of books” with “mass of books” and require a count-versus-measurement explanation.

SESSION 2 — Levels of Measurement

Daily objectives: Classify variables as nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio and justify the classification using properties of the scale.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Sort examples such as blood type, satisfaction rating, temperature in °C, and mass into groups based on what comparisons are meaningful.

ANALYSIS

Reasoning

Ask whether categories have order, equal intervals, and a meaningful zero. Learners explain why “twice as much” is not meaningful for every scale.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling
Nominal: labels only.
Ordinal: ordered categories.
Interval: equal intervals, no true zero.
Ratio: equal intervals and meaningful zero.

APPLICATION

Guided → Independent

Classify school survey variables and defend each classification using at least one property of the measurement level.

Evidence-to-action: If learners confuse ordinal and interval, use temperature in °C versus ranked satisfaction and ask what arithmetic statements are justified.

SESSION 3 — Central Tendency and Variability

Daily objectives: Calculate and compare mean, median, mode, range, and standard deviation for suitable numerical data, then select an appropriate measure for a stated purpose.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Given a small data set, learners predict which “typical value” would best describe it and identify whether an extreme value might affect their choice.

ANALYSIS

Error Analysis

Compare two data sets with the same mean but different spread. Discuss why center alone does not fully describe data.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling
Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
Range = maximum − minimum.
Standard deviation describes the spread of values around the mean.

Model mean, median, mode, range, and interpretation. Treat calculator/software output as evidence to interpret, not merely numbers to copy.

APPLICATION

Guided → Independent

Calculate measures for small data sets and choose the most informative measure of center and variability for each scenario.

Evidence-to-action: If learners automatically choose the mean, provide a data set with an extreme value and ask them to compare mean and median.

SESSION 4 — Technology and Interpretation

Daily objectives: Use an appropriate calculator or spreadsheet to obtain measures of central tendency and variability and interpret the results in a real-world context.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Use a short class data set such as quiz scores or daily study time. Learners identify the variable, measurement level, and likely measures before calculating.

ANALYSIS

Formative Check

Compare manually calculated and technology-generated results. Discuss rounding, data entry, and what each statistic means.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis

Model a complete routine: identify data → identify level/type → choose measure → calculate → inspect center/spread → interpret → check reasonableness.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Interpret a technology-generated summary and make one evidence-based statement about the data. Where technology is unavailable, use a calculator or prepared output.

Evidence-to-action: Classify errors as data classification, measure selection, calculation/data-entry, or interpretation errors, then reteach only the needed component.

LEARNER GUIDE

Understanding Data

From classifying variables to choosing and interpreting statistical measures.

What You Will Learn

  • Types of data.
  • Levels of measurement.
  • Measures of central tendency.
  • Measures of variability.
  • How technology can calculate statistics.
  • How to interpret statistics rather than merely report them.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
Qualitative dataData describing categories or qualities.
Quantitative dataNumerical data representing counts or measurements.
Discrete dataCountable numerical values, commonly whole-number counts.
Continuous dataMeasured numerical values that can take values within a range.
NominalCategories or labels without an inherent order.
OrdinalOrdered categories where rank matters but differences are not necessarily equal.
IntervalNumerical scale with equal intervals but no meaningful absolute zero.
RatioNumerical scale with equal intervals and a meaningful zero.
VariabilityThe extent to which values differ or spread out.

Key Ideas & Formulas

Mean = (sum of all values) ÷ (number of values)
Range = maximum value − minimum value

Median is the middle value after arranging data. If there are two middle values, the median is their average.

Mode is the value or category occurring most frequently.

Standard deviation summarizes how spread out numerical observations are around the mean. A larger standard deviation indicates greater spread, while a smaller one indicates values are more concentrated around the mean.

Worked Examples

1. Type of data

Number of learners absent today is quantitative and discrete because it is a numerical count. Height is quantitative and continuous because it is measured and can take values within a range.

2. Levels of measurement

Class section is nominal. Satisfaction level such as poor/fair/good/excellent is ordinal. Temperature in °C is interval. Mass in kilograms is ratio because zero mass represents absence of mass and ratios are meaningful.

3. Mean, median, mode, and range

For 4, 6, 6, 8, 11: mean = (4+6+6+8+11) ÷ 5 = 35 ÷ 5 = 7. Median = 6. Mode = 6. Range = 11 − 4 = 7.

4. Why interpretation matters

For the data above, saying “the mean is 7” is incomplete. A useful interpretation is: “The average value is 7, while the middle observation is 6 and the values span 7 units from minimum to maximum.”

5. Technology

A calculator or spreadsheet can compute statistics quickly, but the learner still needs to enter the correct data, select the appropriate statistic, check the output, and explain what the result means.

Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Calling every numerical variable discrete.
  • Assuming ordinal data have equal intervals.
  • Using the mean automatically even when an extreme value makes the median more representative.
  • Confusing range with standard deviation.
  • Reporting a calculator result without interpreting it.
  • Entering incorrect or incomplete data into software.

Real-World Connections

Data classification and summary statistics are used in school monitoring, surveys, business decisions, sports, weather reports, quality control, and research. The appropriate statistic depends on the type of data and the question being asked.

Quick Self-Check

  1. What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative data?
  2. Why is a number of siblings discrete?
  3. What makes ordinal data different from nominal data?
  4. Why is temperature in °C commonly treated as interval data?
  5. When might the median be more useful than the mean?
  6. What does a larger standard deviation indicate?
  7. Why should technology output still be interpreted?

Activity Sheet — Complete Solutions

  1. Qualitative. Favorite color is categorical rather than numerical.
  2. Discrete. Siblings are counted in whole-number values.
  3. Ordinal. Ranks have order, but the difference between ranks is not a measured equal interval.
  4. Interval. Celsius temperature has meaningful equal intervals but 0 °C is not an absolute absence of temperature.
  5. Ratio. Kilograms have equal intervals and a meaningful zero.
  6. Mean = (5+7+8+10) ÷ 4 = 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5.
  7. Ordered data: 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12. Median = 6; mode = 5.
  8. Range = 25 − 14 = 11.
  9. Mean = 35 ÷ 5 = 7; median = 6; mode = 6; range = 11 − 4 = 7.
  10. Ordinal. The categories have a natural order, but the differences between categories are not necessarily equal.
  11. Continuous. Time is measured and can take values such as 12.5 minutes.
  12. The second set. Standard deviation 12 indicates greater spread than standard deviation 3, assuming the same units and comparable context.
  13. The zero on the Celsius scale does not represent an absolute absence of temperature; therefore Celsius is treated as interval rather than ratio.
  14. The value 100 is an extreme value that pulls the mean upward. The median is more resistant and may better represent the typical value.
  15. Class A. Standard deviation 2 indicates scores are more closely clustered around the mean than standard deviation 9.
  16. The data are qualitative/categorical and the level is nominal because the categories have no inherent ranking.
  17. Data: 12, 15, 15, 18, 20. Mean = 80 ÷ 5 = 16. Population standard deviation ≈ 2.83; sample standard deviation ≈ 3.54. Accept either only if the learner identifies which convention/calculator setting was used. Interpretation: values are centered at 16 minutes with moderate spread around that center.
  18. Answers vary. Example: 5, 8, 10, 10, 12, 15 has mean 60 ÷ 6 = 10 and range 15−5 = 10. Greater spread means values are more dispersed than a set such as 9, 10, 10, 10, 10, 11.

Assessment — Complete Solutions

  1. B. Number of books is a numerical count, so it is quantitative and discrete.
  2. B. Ordinal. Ordered categories are ordinal.
  3. Mean = (6+8+10+12) ÷ 4 = 36 ÷ 4 = 9.
  4. Range = 15 − 4 = 11.
  5. Median. The median is generally more resistant to extreme values than the mean.
  6. Set Y. Standard deviation 8 indicates more spread than standard deviation 2.
  7. Ordinal. Poor, fair, good, and excellent have a natural order, but the distances between categories are not established as equal.
  8. Mean = (20+25+25+30+50) ÷ 5 = 150 ÷ 5 = 30 minutes. Median = 25 minutes. Mode = 25 minutes. Range = 50−20 = 30 minutes. Because 50 is relatively high compared with the other values, the median of 25 minutes is a reasonable typical value; it is less affected by the extreme value than the mean.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Thinking every numerical variable is discrete.
  • Confusing ordinal with interval data.
  • Assuming a numerical zero automatically makes a variable ratio.
  • Choosing the mean without examining the data distribution or extreme values.
  • Confusing range with standard deviation.
  • Treating technology output as an answer without interpretation.

Remediation

  • Use a classification decision tree for qualitative/quantitative and discrete/continuous.
  • Use the four measurement levels with repeated concrete examples.
  • Contrast paired examples: rank vs temperature, temperature vs mass.
  • Use small data sets before introducing calculator or spreadsheet output.
  • Provide “same mean, different spread” examples to establish the purpose of variability.
  • Require every statistical answer to include a number plus a sentence of interpretation.

Enrichment

  • Collect a small class data set and justify the most appropriate statistics.
  • Compare mean and median before and after adding an extreme value.
  • Use Excel, Jamovi, or a calculator to verify manual computations.
  • Create a short data story in which a statistic supports a practical decision.
