SESSION 1 — Surface Area of Prisms and Pyramids

Daily objective: Determine the surface area of rectangular prisms and pyramids by identifying all required faces and dimensions.

ACTIVITY

Elicit

Display a box and a square-based pyramid. Learners identify faces, edges, and dimensions, then predict which surfaces would need covering if the solid were wrapped.

ANALYSIS

Formative Check

Compare a net of a rectangular prism with the solid. Discuss why surface area counts the areas of all exposed faces. For a pyramid, distinguish base area from the triangular lateral faces.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling

For a rectangular prism, surface area is the sum of the areas of its six faces. For a right prism, SA = 2B + Ph, where B is base area, P is base perimeter, and h is prism height. For a pyramid, SA = B + ½Pl, where l is slant height for a regular/right pyramid.

Rectangular prism: SA = 2(lw + lh + wh)
Regular pyramid: SA = B + ½Pl

APPLICATION

Guided → Independent

Find the surface area of rectangular prisms and square pyramids. Learners label dimensions, select a formula, substitute, calculate, and state square units.

Evidence-to-action: If learners omit a face, return to a net or physical model. If the correct faces are identified but arithmetic is inaccurate, isolate the arithmetic step for short practice.

SESSION 2 — Surface Area of Cylinders, Cones, and Spheres

Daily objective: Calculate surface area of cylinders, cones, and spheres and distinguish total surface area from lateral/curved surface area when appropriate.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Examine a can, cone-shaped object, and ball. Learners identify circular bases and curved surfaces and predict which parts would be covered.

ANALYSIS

Error Analysis

Compare a learner's use of 2πr² + 2πrh for a closed cylinder with an incorrect formula that omits a base. Discuss whether the context requires a closed or open solid.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling

Use the standard total surface-area formulas. For a closed cylinder, SA = 2πr² + 2πrh. For a cone, SA = πr² + πrl. For a sphere, SA = 4πr², where l is slant height of the cone.

Cylinder: SA = 2πr² + 2πrh
Cone: SA = πr² + πrl
Sphere: SA = 4πr²

APPLICATION

Practice

Solve surface-area problems involving a closed cylinder, cone, and sphere. Use π ≈ 3.14 unless another value is specified. Round sensibly and include square units.

Teacher response: When learners confuse radius and diameter, have them mark the centre-to-edge distance before substitution and check whether r = d ÷ 2 is needed.

SESSION 3 — Volume of Prisms, Pyramids, Cylinders, Cones, and Spheres

Daily objective: Calculate the volume of common solid figures and explain how the formula relates to the solid's dimensions.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Sort solids into prism/pyramid/curved categories. Learners predict which shapes could be filled with equal layers and which use a fraction of a corresponding prism/cylinder.

ANALYSIS

Formative Check

Compare V = Bh for a prism with V = ⅓Bh for a pyramid. For curved solids, compare cylinder/cone and sphere formulas. Ask learners to identify which dimensions belong in each formula.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit Modeling
Prism: V = Bh
Pyramid: V = ⅓Bh
Cylinder: V = πr²h
Cone: V = ⅓πr²h
Sphere: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³

Volume measures the amount of three-dimensional space occupied and is expressed in cubic units.

APPLICATION

Guided → Independent

Calculate volumes of a rectangular prism, square pyramid, cylinder, cone, and sphere. Learners identify B or r first, then substitute and label cubic units.

Evidence-to-action: If learners choose the wrong formula, use a formula-sort task before further computation. If formula selection is secure but errors remain, focus on substitution and calculator entry.

SESSION 4 — Composite Solids and Real-Life Applications

Daily objective: Solve surface-area and volume problems involving composite solids, justify the selected approach, and interpret the result in context.

ACTIVITY

Retrieval

Review formula cards. Learners decompose a composite solid into familiar figures, identifying shared or hidden surfaces.

ANALYSIS

Reasoning

Analyze which surfaces should be counted for an outside-painting problem and which internal/shared surfaces should not. Compare addition and subtraction of component volumes.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis
Composite volume = add/subtract component volumes as required.
Composite surface area = add only the exposed surfaces required by the context.

Always sketch, label, decompose, choose formulas, calculate, check, and interpret.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Solve real-life packaging, storage, painting, construction, and capacity problems. Learners explain why a surface is included or excluded and report appropriate units.

Evidence-to-action: Classify learners as Not Yet Mastered, Developing, or Mastered. Reteach the exact failed step—figure recognition, formula selection, exposed-surface reasoning, computation, or interpretation—then reassess.

LEARNER GUIDE

Surface Area and Volume

A practical guide to measuring the outside and inside of three-dimensional objects.

What You Will Learn

  • How to determine surface area of common solid figures.
  • How to calculate volume of common solid figures.
  • How to handle composite solids.
  • How to select formulas, substitute correctly, use appropriate units, and check answers.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
Surface areaThe total area of the exposed surfaces of a three-dimensional object.
VolumeThe amount of three-dimensional space occupied by a solid.
Base area (B)The area of the base of a prism or pyramid.
Radius (r)The distance from the centre of a circle or sphere to its edge.
Slant height (l)The sloping height used in the lateral surface-area formula of a regular/right pyramid or cone.
Composite solidA solid formed by combining two or more familiar solid figures.

Formula Guide

SolidSurface AreaVolume
Rectangular prismSA = 2(lw + lh + wh)V = lwh
PrismSA = 2B + PhV = Bh
Regular pyramidSA = B + ½PlV = ⅓Bh
Closed cylinderSA = 2πr² + 2πrhV = πr²h
ConeSA = πr² + πrlV = ⅓πr²h
SphereSA = 4πr²V = ⁴⁄₃πr³

Step-by-Step Worked Examples

1. Rectangular Prism — Surface Area

A box has l = 8 cm, w = 5 cm, and h = 3 cm.

SA = 2(lw + lh + wh)
= 2(8×5 + 8×3 + 5×3)
= 2(40 + 24 + 15)
= 158 cm².

2. Cylinder — Volume

A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Use π ≈ 3.14.

V = πr²h = 3.14(3²)(10) = 3.14(9)(10) = 282.6 cm³.

3. Cone — Surface Area

A cone has r = 3 cm and l = 5 cm.

SA = πr² + πrl = 3.14(9) + 3.14(15) = 28.26 + 47.10 = 75.36 cm².

4. Sphere — Volume

A sphere has radius 3 cm.

V = ⁴⁄₃πr³ = ⁴⁄₃(3.14)(27) = 113.04 cm³.

5. Composite Volume

A solid is formed by a 10 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm rectangular prism and a 4 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm rectangular prism attached without overlap.

V₁ = 10×4×3 = 120 cm³.
V₂ = 4×4×3 = 48 cm³.
Total V = 120 + 48 = 168 cm³.

Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Using a surface-area formula when the question asks for volume, or vice versa.
  • Using diameter as radius without dividing by 2.
  • Forgetting a base when the solid is closed.
  • Counting a hidden/shared surface in a composite outside-surface problem.
  • Using square units for volume or cubic units for area.
  • Rounding too early.

Real-World Connections

Surface area is useful for painting, wrapping, coating, packaging, and estimating material needed to cover an object. Volume is useful for storage capacity, filling containers, construction, manufacturing, and estimating how much material a solid can hold or occupy.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Confusing surface area with volume.
  • Using diameter as radius.
  • Choosing a formula based only on appearance rather than the solid.
  • Forgetting a base or counting an internal/shared surface.
  • Confusing slant height with vertical height in cone/pyramid surface-area problems.
  • Using square units for volume or cubic units for surface area.

Remediation

  • Use physical solids and nets before symbolic formulas.
  • Build a formula-to-solid matching routine.
  • Have learners label every dimension before substituting.
  • For circles, explicitly identify radius versus diameter.
  • Use worked-example comparison: correct solution versus one-error solution.
  • Require a final “Does this unit make sense?” check.

Enrichment

  • Design packaging that minimizes surface area for a fixed volume.
  • Compare two container designs with equal volume but different surface areas.
  • Model a composite object from classroom materials and calculate its volume.
  • Use technology or spreadsheets to investigate how changing one dimension affects surface area and volume.
