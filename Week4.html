Learning Intentions

Learning Objectives

At the end of the five-session learning sequence, learners should be able to:

  1. Define and distinguish business loans from consumer loans based on purpose, repayment terms, requirements, and interest considerations.
  2. Identify the principal, interest rate, payment frequency, loan term, and number of payments in a loan problem.
  3. Calculate regular loan payments using an amortization formula.
  4. Interpret and compare loan options and justify a financially reasonable borrowing decision.

1. Business Loans and Consumer Loans

Business Loan

A business loan is money borrowed primarily to support a business activity. It may be used to purchase equipment, increase inventory, improve a store, or provide working capital.

Consumer Loan

A consumer loan is generally used by an individual or household to finance personal purchases or expenses, such as an appliance, vehicle, or other personal need.

Feature Business Loan Consumer Loan
Primary purpose Business activity Personal or household use
Possible use Equipment, inventory, expansion Appliance, vehicle, personal purchase
Repayment May be structured according to business cash flow Usually scheduled regular payments
Requirements May include business and financial documents May include proof of income and identification

2. Important Loan Terms

Term Meaning
Principal The amount originally borrowed.
Interest rate The rate charged for borrowing money.
Term The length of time allowed to repay the loan.
Payment The amount regularly paid by the borrower.
Amortization The gradual repayment of a loan through scheduled payments.
Outstanding balance The amount of principal that remains unpaid.
Total interest The total amount paid for borrowing the money.

3. Amortization Formula

For a loan with equal periodic payments, the regular payment may be calculated using:

A = P × [r(1 + r)ⁿ] ÷ [(1 + r)ⁿ − 1]

Where:

  • A = regular payment
  • P = principal or amount borrowed
  • r = interest rate per payment period expressed as a decimal
  • n = total number of payments
The interest rate and number of payments must use the same time period. For monthly payments, use the monthly interest rate and the total number of monthly payments.

4. Worked Example 1: Basic Loan Payment

Problem: A borrower takes a ₱120,000 loan at 12% annual interest, compounded monthly, to be paid monthly for 1 year. Find the monthly payment.
Step 1: Identify the principal.
P = ₱120,000
Step 2: Convert the annual rate to a monthly rate.
r = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01
Step 3: Determine the number of payments.
n = 1 × 12 = 12
Step 4: Substitute into the formula.
A = ₱120,000 × [0.01(1.01)¹²] ÷ [(1.01)¹² − 1]
Step 5: Calculate.
A ≈ ₱10,661.85
Monthly payment ≈ ₱10,661.85

5. Worked Example 2: Total Payment and Total Interest

Suppose the monthly payment is ₱10,661.85 for 12 months.

Total payment = ₱10,661.85 × 12
Total payment ≈ ₱127,942.20
Total interest = Total payment − Principal
Total interest ≈ ₱127,942.20 − ₱120,000
Total interest ≈ ₱7,942.20

6. Worked Example 3: Comparing Two Loans

A borrower needs ₱100,000. Lender A offers a monthly payment of ₱4,700 for 24 months. Lender B offers ₱3,900 for 30 months.

Lender A Lender B
Monthly payment ₱4,700 ₱3,900
Number of payments 24 30
Total paid ₱112,800 ₱117,000
Total interest ₱12,800 ₱17,000

Lender B has the lower monthly payment, but the borrower pays more overall. This illustrates why loan decisions should consider both affordability and total cost.

7. Mortgage

A mortgage is a loan commonly used to finance real property such as a house. Mortgage payments are generally made over a long period and may include principal and interest.

In practical decision-making, a borrower should consider the loan amount, interest rate, payment schedule, loan term, total interest, fees, and the borrower's ability to make payments.

8. Common Mistakes

  1. Using the annual interest rate directly when payments are monthly.
  2. Using the number of years instead of the total number of payments.
  3. Forgetting that the periodic rate must be expressed as a decimal.
  4. Comparing loans using monthly payment alone.
  5. Forgetting to subtract the principal when calculating total interest.
  6. Rounding too early in the calculation.

Before official classroom use, verify the final numerical values with a calculator or spreadsheet, particularly when displaying rounded amortization payments. Intermediate values should not be rounded prematurely.

Teacher Notes

Teaching Focus

The central goal is not merely formula substitution. Learners should understand what the numbers represent and use them to make reasonable financial decisions.

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Confusing principal with total amount paid.
  • Using the annual rate when monthly payments are required.
  • Using the number of years instead of the total number of payment periods.
  • Believing the smallest monthly payment is always the best option.
  • Confusing total payment with total interest.
  • Rounding intermediate calculations too early.

Suggested Remediation

  1. Use a four-column organizer: Principal | Periodic Rate | Number of Payments | Payment.
  2. Begin with identifying variables before requiring formula substitution.
  3. Use a calculator demonstration showing exactly how powers such as (1 + r)ⁿ are entered.
  4. Give learners partially completed examples and gradually remove the scaffolding.
  5. Ask learners to explain each number in words after every calculation.

Enrichment

Ask advanced learners to construct a simple loan comparison table using spreadsheet software. They may vary the principal, interest rate, and repayment period and observe how each variable affects the payment and total interest.

Learners may also investigate the first few rows of an amortization schedule and identify how the portion of each payment allocated to interest changes as the balance decreases.
