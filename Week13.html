Weekly Learning Intentions

  • Identify opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse sides relative to a chosen acute angle.
  • Use sine, cosine, and tangent to determine unknown sides or angles in right-angled triangles.
  • Illustrate and calculate the area of an oblique triangle using Heron’s Formula.
  • Calculate the area of an oblique triangle using A = ½ab sin C and select the appropriate method.
Evidence-informed cycle: retrieve prior geometry → predict → analyze errors → explicitly model → guided practice → independent practice → application → mastery check → targeted remediation.

SESSION 1 — Trigonometric Ratios in Right Triangles

Daily objectives: Identify the three sides relative to an acute angle and determine sine, cosine, and tangent ratios from a right triangle or real-life figure.

ACTIVITY

Elicit prior knowledge

Show a right-triangle ramp or ladder. Learners label the hypotenuse, opposite, and adjacent sides relative to a marked angle, then predict which sides should be compared.

ANALYSIS

Formative check

Compare labels from pairs. Ask: “Adjacent to which angle?” and “Which side is always opposite the right angle?” Address the common error of treating adjacent as the longest side.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling
sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent

Model how the chosen reference angle determines the opposite and adjacent sides.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Learners identify sides and write the correct ratio for several right triangles, including a ladder and ramp context.

Evidence-to-action: If learners swap opposite and adjacent, return to “relative to the chosen angle” with color-free labeling and a new diagram before computation.

SESSION 2 — Solving Right Triangles

Daily objectives: Use an appropriate trigonometric ratio to calculate an unknown side or acute angle and check whether the result is reasonable.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Give a right triangle with one acute angle and one known side. Learners choose whether sine, cosine, or tangent can connect the known and unknown sides.

ANALYSIS

Choice reasoning

Compare solution paths. Learners explain why a ratio is selected based on the side pair rather than choosing a formula from memory alone.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling

Model selecting the ratio → substituting values → calculator mode check → solving → rounding → reasonableness check. For unknown angles, model inverse trigonometric functions.

θ = sin⁻¹(opposite/hypotenuse), when appropriate
θ = cos⁻¹(adjacent/hypotenuse), when appropriate
θ = tan⁻¹(opposite/adjacent), when appropriate

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Solve ladder, ramp, and line-of-sight problems. Require a diagram, ratio choice, computation, and unit/angle statement.

Evidence-to-action: If learners obtain an impossible side longer than the hypotenuse, use a triangle-size check before repeating the computation.

SESSION 3 — Heron’s Formula for Oblique Triangles

Daily objectives: Identify when all three sides are known, calculate the semiperimeter, and use Heron’s Formula to determine the area of an oblique triangle.

ACTIVITY

Connect

Present a triangular garden with side lengths 13 m, 14 m, and 15 m. Learners discuss how area could be found when no altitude is supplied.

ANALYSIS

Strategy selection

Compare the available information with the formulas from Sessions 1–2. Identify that three side lengths are sufficient for Heron’s Formula.

ABSTRACTION

Explicit modeling
s = (a+b+c) ÷ 2
A = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)]

Model the sequence: identify sides → find s → substitute → simplify → square root → state square units.

APPLICATION

Guided → independent

Find the area of several oblique triangles from side lengths. Learners show the semiperimeter and complete substitution before using a calculator.

Evidence-to-action: If learners omit the semiperimeter or substitute the wrong side, require a four-line solution template before another item.

SESSION 4 — Area = ½ab sin C and Method Selection

Daily objectives: Use A = ½ab sin C to find an oblique triangle’s area, determine which area method fits the given information, and justify the result in context.

ACTIVITY

Retrieve

Compare two triangles: one provides two sides and the included angle; another provides three sides. Learners choose the likely area method before calculating.

ANALYSIS

Error analysis

Examine a solution that uses a non-included angle. Learners explain why the angle must be between the two known sides in A = ½ab sin C.

ABSTRACTION

Synthesis
A = ½ab sin C

Model method selection: three sides → Heron; two sides plus included angle → ½ab sin C. Review calculator angle mode and square units.

APPLICATION

Mastery

Solve mixed real-world problems involving triangular land, triangular supports, and triangular panels. Explain why the selected formula fits the information.

Evidence-to-action: Classify mistakes as side/angle identification, formula selection, calculator mode, arithmetic, or interpretation errors, then provide targeted practice.

LEARNER GUIDE

Trigonometry and Oblique Triangle Areas

Choose the right relationship, calculate carefully, and explain what the answer means.

What You Will Learn

  • How sine, cosine, and tangent describe relationships in right triangles.
  • How to solve for missing sides and angles.
  • How Heron’s Formula finds area when three sides are known.
  • How A = ½ab sin C finds area when two sides and their included angle are known.
  • How to select a method from the information given.

Essential Definitions

TermMeaning
Right triangleA triangle containing a 90° angle.
HypotenuseThe side opposite the 90° angle; it is the longest side of a right triangle.
Opposite sideThe side directly across from the chosen acute reference angle.
Adjacent sideThe non-hypotenuse side next to the chosen reference angle.
Oblique triangleA triangle that is not a right triangle.
Included angleThe angle formed by the two known sides used in A = ½ab sin C.
SemiperimeterHalf the perimeter of a triangle: s = (a+b+c) ÷ 2.

Key Ratios & Formulas

sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent
s = (a+b+c) ÷ 2
Heron: A = √[s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)]
Area of oblique triangle: A = ½ab sin C

Method selection: If three side lengths are given, Heron’s Formula is appropriate. If two side lengths and their included angle are given, use A = ½ab sin C.

Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Example 1 — Identify the ratio

A right triangle has θ = 35°. Relative to θ, the opposite side is 6 cm and the hypotenuse is 10 cm. Since opposite and hypotenuse are involved, use sine: sin 35° = 6/10 = 0.6. This identifies the relationship being represented.

Example 2 — Find a side

A ladder forms a 60° angle with the ground. The ladder is 5 m long. Let h be the vertical height. Since h is opposite the 60° angle and 5 m is the hypotenuse:

sin 60° = h/5
h = 5 sin 60° ≈ 4.33 m

The vertical height is approximately 4.33 m.

Example 3 — Heron’s Formula

For a triangle with sides 13 m, 14 m, and 15 m:

s = (13+14+15) ÷ 2 = 21 m
A = √[21(21−13)(21−14)(21−15)]
= √(21×8×7×6) = √7056 = 84 m²

Example 4 — Two sides and included angle

If a triangular panel has sides 8 m and 11 m with an included angle of 30°:

A = ½(8)(11)sin 30°
= 44(0.5) = 22 m²

The area is 22 m².

Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Calling the side next to the angle “adjacent” without checking that it is not the hypotenuse.
  • Using the wrong trigonometric ratio because the side pair was not identified first.
  • Using degrees/radians incorrectly on a calculator.
  • For A = ½ab sin C, using an angle that is not included between a and b.
  • Forgetting to calculate the semiperimeter in Heron’s Formula.
  • Reporting triangle area in linear units instead of square units.
  • Rounding too early.

Real-World Connections

Trigonometry helps estimate heights, distances, slopes, roof angles, ladder positions, and line-of-sight measurements. Triangle-area methods can be used for land plots, triangular signs, panels, supports, and construction layouts.

Quick Self-Check

  1. Which side is always the hypotenuse?
  2. When should sine be used?
  3. What is the difference between an adjacent side and the hypotenuse?
  4. When is Heron’s Formula useful?
  5. What does “included angle” mean?
  6. Why must the calculator be in the correct angle mode?
  7. Why is area expressed in square units?

Activity Sheet — Complete Solutions

  1. Hypotenuse. It is opposite the 90° angle.
  2. sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse; cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse; tan θ = opposite/adjacent.
  3. Sine. The known pair is opposite and hypotenuse.
  4. s = (7+8+9) ÷ 2 = 24 ÷ 2 = 12 cm.
  5. A = ½ab sin C.
  6. Angle C must be the included angle between sides a and b.
  7. sin 30° = opposite/12. Opposite = 12 sin 30° = 12(0.5) = 6 cm.
  8. cos 40° = 9/h. h = 9 ÷ cos 40° ≈ 9 ÷ 0.7660 = 11.75 m ≈ 11.7 m.
  9. tan θ = 7/10. θ = tan⁻¹(0.7) ≈ 35.0°.
  10. s = (13+14+15) ÷ 2 = 21. A = √[21(8)(7)(6)] = √7056 = 84 m².
  11. A = ½(8)(11)sin 30° = 44(0.5) = 22 cm².
  12. A = ½(12)(15)sin 50° = 90(0.766044) ≈ 68.94 m² ≈ 68.9 m².
  13. The learner reversed the sine definition. cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, while sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse.
  14. A = ½ab sin C requires C to be the angle formed by the two sides a and b. If 40° is not between them, the formula cannot be applied with those two sides and that angle as stated.
  15. s = (9+10+17) ÷ 2 = 36 ÷ 2 = 18, not 17.
  16. sin 70° = h/6. h = 6 sin 70° ≈ 6(0.939693) = 5.6 m.
  17. s = (20+21+29) ÷ 2 = 35. A = √[35(15)(14)(6)] = √44100 = 210 m².
  18. Use Heron’s Formula when all three side lengths are known. Use A = ½ab sin C when two sides and their included angle are known.

Assessment — Complete Solutions

  1. C. Sine. sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse.
  2. B. 0.5. sin θ = 5/10 = 0.5.
  3. sin 30° = h/10 → h = 10(0.5) = 5 m.
  4. Heron’s Formula. Three side lengths are given.
  5. s = (13+14+15) ÷ 2 = 21. A = √[21(8)(7)(6)] = 84 square units.
  6. A = ½(8)(11)sin 30° = 22 m².
  7. The formula comes from the geometry of a triangle in which the height relative to side a is b sin C. Therefore C must be the angle between a and b.
  8. A = ½(12)(15)sin 50° = 90(0.766044) = 68.9 m² to the nearest tenth. This is reasonable because a triangle formed by two sides 12 m and 15 m has maximum possible area 90 m² when the included angle is 90°; 50° gives a smaller area.

TEACHER NOTES

Support, Remediation and Enrichment

Common Learner Misconceptions

  • Confusing opposite and adjacent because they are relative to the selected angle.
  • Calling the hypotenuse the adjacent side.
  • Selecting a trig ratio before identifying the known and unknown sides.
  • Using the wrong calculator angle mode.
  • Using a non-included angle in A = ½ab sin C.
  • Forgetting the semiperimeter in Heron’s Formula.
  • Using linear rather than square units for area.

Remediation

  • Use a consistent “mark the angle → label O/A/H → choose ratio” routine.
  • Provide partially completed triangles before full word problems.
  • Use one calculator-mode check at the start of every computation session.
  • For Heron, require the sequence “s → four factors → square root → square units.”
  • For ½ab sin C, highlight the included angle and the two corresponding sides.
  • Use error-analysis tasks before assigning additional repetitive computation.

Enrichment

  • Estimate the height of a school object using a measured angle and distance.
  • Compare two possible methods for finding a triangle area and justify the more efficient method.
  • Design a triangular garden or panel and calculate its area using realistic measurements.
  • Use a spreadsheet or geometry software to verify selected results and discuss rounding.
